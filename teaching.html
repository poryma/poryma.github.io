Teaching

Courses I teach and have taught, with outlines for Korea University courses, and the ideas behind how I teach.

Teaching Philosophy

I've taught at an open university, a state research university, a selective liberal arts college, and now Korea University, for more than a decade. The students have changed a lot from place to place. What I try to do with them hasn't.

Sociology as a way of seeing

I want students to leave able to look at a headline about suicide, housing, or a disaster and ask who is affected, why, and what the evidence actually says. Theory earns its place in my classes when it helps explain something real, so every course connects ideas to a current Korean or global problem.

Learning by doing, with real data

Students learn more from one messy dataset than from ten clean examples. They map regional suicide rates, run regressions on income and mental health, write policy briefs as if they worked in disaster management, and compare Korea's health insurance with the U.S. system using public data. Most of my courses have a computer lab component, whatever the topic.

A classroom everyone can follow

Many of my students learn sociology in their second language. In the U.S. I taught refugees, transfer students, and first-generation students who had never run a statistic. So I give structured, specific feedback, explain hard concepts more than one way, and add glossaries and bilingual support where they help. Students often say this is what gave them confidence with quantitative work.

Teaching with, and about, AI

AI is already in my students' lives, so I'd rather teach them to use it well than pretend it isn't there. In class they do the same task once without AI and once with it, then check what they actually learned. They verify AI-generated references and audit models for bias. The goal is judgment, not dependence.

Always revising

I run a mid-semester survey in every course and change things based on what students tell me. I completed the ACUE certificate in effective teaching practices and keep redesigning courses, most recently into flipped SEMO classes. In Spring 2026 two of my courses received Korea University's Seoktop Teaching Award, which I share with the students who did the work.

Thinking Like a Sociologist in the Age of AINewUndergraduate · Public Sociology · English · new course, in development+

What should students hand over to AI, what must they keep for themselves, and how can they tell the difference? Each week pairs a classic and a recent study with an in-class task done once without AI and once with it.

Course flow

  • What we hand over: cognitive offloading, illusions of understanding, metacognition
  • What we keep: deskilling, tutoring, memory, trust and verification
  • Studying society with AI: "silicon samples" and AI coding of texts; writing your own AI principles

Signature activities

  • AI-off vs. AI-on contrast activities, with class results shown in real time
  • DOI hunt: sorting AI-generated references into real, misdescribed, or fabricated
Seminar on Learning and Research in the Age of AINewGraduate seminar · Department of Social Welfare · English · new course, in development+

A seminar for thesis writers on which parts of research to hand to AI, which to keep, and how to know the work is still sound and still yours, with particular attention to confidential data in social welfare research.

Course flow

  • Learning: self-regulated learning, cognitive offloading, when AI harms learning
  • Research workflow: automation and deskilling, personal knowledge bases, trust and verification
  • Practice: literature review, qualitative and confidential data, writing and authorship

Signature activities

  • Abstract screening done by hand and with AI, compared with Cohen's kappa
  • A personal research workflow plan: what to hand to AI, what to keep, how to verify

AI and Society (PUBS279)NewUndergraduate · Public Sociology · English · new course+

An introduction to how technology and society shape each other, read through Science and Technology Studies (STS) frameworks such as the social construction of technology, actor-network theory, and the politics of artifacts. The course traces this relationship from print and industrialization to the digital revolution, then examines how AI operates in bias, surveillance, work, health care, criminal justice, media, creativity, and education. It asks how AI produces unequal effects across class, gender, race, and region, and what fair governance of AI would require.

Course flow

  • Weeks 1–4: Technology and society in history: print, industrialization, STS frameworks, risk and network society
  • Weeks 5–7: What AI is; algorithmic bias; surveillance and privacy
  • Week 8: Midterm
  • Weeks 9–12: AI and work, health care and criminal justice, social media and democracy, creativity and copyright
  • Weeks 13–15: AI and education, the global digital divide and AI governance, AI and the environment
  • Week 16: Final exam

Signature activities

  • Bias audit workshop: testing AI image generators and text classifiers for gender, racial, and cultural bias
  • Algorithmic decision-making simulation with the COMPAS dataset to examine racial disparities in risk scores
  • Multilingual AI inequality audit: identical prompts in English, Korean, and a low-resource language to measure gaps in response quality
  • 'Big Other' self-audit of students' own ad personalization settings, app permissions, and location history
Seminar on Inequality of Disaster (SOW707)Graduate seminar (master's and doctoral) · Department of Social Welfare+

A graduate seminar that treats disasters and crises as social welfare problems. It examines how natural hazards (earthquakes, floods, pandemics) and social crises (war and violence, financial shocks, climate change) distribute risk, loss, and recovery unequally by class, gender, race, and region, and how welfare states, service delivery systems, and public policy shape those outcomes. Cases from South Korea, the United States, and the Global South are compared to ask how research can inform more equitable disaster governance.

Course flow

  • Weeks 1–4: Conceptual foundations: what a disaster is, vulnerability and resilience, the development of disaster research, structural explanations
  • Weeks 5–7: Financial crisis; hurricanes; floods and environmental justice
  • Week 8: Research proposal week
  • Weeks 9–12: Earthquakes and tsunamis; pandemics; violence and armed conflict; climate change
  • Weeks 13–14: Inequalities in disasters by class, poverty, race, and gender
  • Weeks 15–16: Student research presentations; final paper

Signature activities

  • Weekly one-page reflection memos on the required readings
  • Reading-led discussion: each required reading is assigned to one student, who opens its discussion with a short presentation
  • Short research proposal (3-5 pages) on disaster and inequality
  • Final research paper (15-20 pages), presented in progress in Week 15
Korean Society (PUBS415)Undergraduate · Public Sociology · English · also taught Fall 2025 · Fall 2026 as a flipped SEMO class+

A sociological study of contemporary South Korea built around the concept of compressed modernity, the unusually condensed pace of Korea's transformation. Each week pairs Korean research with comparative or theoretical work on a current tension (fairness and meritocracy, labor market dualization, housing, the world's lowest fertility, gender and political divides among the young, online life and AI, loneliness, K-culture, disaster, migration, democracy) and asks whether Korea is exceptional or part of a global pattern. In Fall 2026 the course runs as a flipped SEMO Class: concepts are learned online before class, and class time is used for debate and evidence workshops.

Course flow

  • Weeks 1–3: Orientation; compressed modernity; meritocracy and fairness (gongjeong)
  • Weeks 4–7: Work and platform labor; housing and generational inequality; low fertility; gender and political divides
  • Week 8: Midterm
  • Weeks 9–12: Digital society; AI; mental health and loneliness; K-culture
  • Weeks 13–15: Risk society and disaster; migration and diversity; democracy from the candlelight protests to the 2024 martial law crisis
  • Week 16: Final exam

Signature activities

  • Housing cost simulation: estimating years-to-purchase for different birth cohorts from public price data
  • Accountability timeline: reconstructing the decision chain of the Sewol or Itaewon disaster
  • Gender gap data workshop: testing AI-generated rival explanations against evidence
  • Weekly half-page reflection abstracts and small-group discussion-board debate

Sociology of Health and Medicine (PUBS387)Seoktop Teaching AwardUndergraduate · Public Sociology · English · 4.92 / 5 · returning Spring 2027 as a flipped SEMO class+

Treats health, illness, and health care as social phenomena, focusing on how social class, gender, race and ethnicity, and place shape health inequalities. Students compare health care systems and policies, especially in South Korea and the United States, and use a data-informed sociological lens to connect structures of inequality to health outcomes and to policy options for reducing disparities.

Course flow

  • Weeks 1–4: Medical sociology: origins and development; social construction of illness and social determinants of health
  • Weeks 5–7: Health inequality and equity; social capital; health insurance systems
  • Week 8: Midterm
  • Weeks 9–12: Education, lifestyle, and life-course cumulative disadvantage in health
  • Weeks 13–15: Gender, race, and health; COVID-19 and health inequality
  • Week 16: Final exam

Signature activities

  • Computer-lab in-class activities
  • Case-based discussion and writing assignments proposing policy alternatives to reduce health disparities

Research Methods on Social Welfare (PUBS463)Undergraduate · English+

A foundation course in social science research methods, with a focus on sociological research applied to social welfare. Students work through the research process: choosing a topic, building a theoretical framework, research ethics, conceptualization and measurement, sampling, experimental, survey, qualitative, and unobtrusive designs, and basic quantitative analysis, while practicing how to find and read qualitative and quantitative studies.

Course flow

  • Weeks 1–4: Research in everyday life; paradigms and theory; ethics and politics of research; research design
  • Weeks 5–7: Conceptualization and measurement; sampling; indexes, scales, and typologies
  • Week 8: Midterm
  • Weeks 9–12: Experiments; survey research; qualitative research; unobtrusive, content-analysis, and evaluation research
  • Weeks 13–15: Quantitative data analysis I-II; writing a research report
  • Week 16: Final exam

Signature activities

  • Weekly quizzes applying that week's methods
  • Research proposal with a short literature review and planned analysis methods
  • In-class assignments during lecture and lab sessions
Seminar on Quality of LifeSeminar · English+

A seminar on quality of life and subjective well-being: how they are conceptualized and measured, and how economic conditions, health, work, family, community, diversity, environment, culture, aging, and crises such as COVID-19 shape them. Early readings cover classic quality-of-life frameworks (e.g., Diener, Veenhoven, Cummins, Costanza).

Course flow

  • Weeks 1–3: Introduction; concepts and frameworks of quality of life; measuring life satisfaction and subjective well-being
  • Weeks 4–7: Economic and material well-being; physical and mental health; work and work-life balance; family and relationships
  • Week 8: Midterm
  • Weeks 9–12: Community and social capital; diversity, ethnicity, and gender; environment and housing; culture
  • Weeks 13–14: Aging and the life course; COVID-19 and other shocks
